# Enzymatic Inactivation of Antibiotics

**Enzymatic Inactivation** is one of the most clinically impactful mechanisms of bacterial antimicrobial resistance. Rather than altering host targets or expelling the drug, the resistant bacterium synthesizes enzymes that chemically alter or destroy the drug molecule before it can reach its intracellular or periplasmic target.

## Biochemical Pathways

### 1. Hydrolytic Cleavage
The most prominent example is the hydrolysis of **beta-lactams** by beta-lactamases:
- **Serine Beta-Lactamases (Ambler Classes A, C, D)**: Utilize an active-site serine (e.g., Ser70 in [[blaTEM]]) to launch a nucleophilic attack on the carbonyl carbon of the beta-lactam ring, forming a transient acyl-enzyme intermediate followed by hydrolysis.
- **Metallo-Beta-Lactamases (Ambler Class B)**: Utilize divalent zinc ions ($Zn^{2+}$) to activate a nucleophilic hydroxide ion that directly attacks the beta-lactam ring.

### 2. Group Transfer (Chemical Modification)
Transferase enzymes append bulky chemical substituents that sterically prevent the antibiotic from docking with its ribosomal or enzymatic target:
- **Phosphorylation**: Aminoglycoside phosphotransferases (APH).
- **Acetylation**: Aminoglycoside acetyltransferases (AAC), chloramphenicol acetyltransferase (CAT).
- **Adenylylation/Nucleotidylation**: Aminoglycoside nucleotidyltransferases (ANT).

## Representative Genes & Determinants
- **Beta-Lactamase**: [[blaTEM]] (TEM-1 serine beta-lactamase).
- Other notable families: `blaSHV`, `blaCTX-M`, `blaKPC`, `blaNDM`.
